\chapter{Introduction}
\label{cap:introduccion}

Cryptoasset markets have a particular feature compared with markets organized solely through order books: the same asset is traded simultaneously on centralized exchanges (CEX) and on decentralized exchanges (DEX), whose prices result from the reserves of a liquidity pool. The two prices do not coincide automatically. Their difference opens arbitrage opportunities, but whether it is corrected depends on the conditions under which trading is possible: available capital, costs and pool depth. The research problem consists in understanding how arbitrage capacity, available depth and participants' decisions change the way a constant-product automated market maker (AMM) tracks a reference price, and in establishing whether a reduced dynamical representation describes the trajectories those mechanisms generate.

The problem matters because two pools with the same marginal price can react very differently to the same order. If liquidity decreases, a fixed-size transaction produces a larger impact; if providers withdraw reserves upon observing a discrepancy or volatility, their own reaction can change the response to later perturbations. The quality of price tracking and the impact of orders are relevant properties of how a market works even when the aim is neither to predict an asset's price nor to optimize an investment strategy. From the standpoint of systems engineering, the interest is in relating local mechanisms, states and constraints to aggregate behavior.

The literature has studied artificial markets with heterogeneous agents, the mathematical properties of AMMs and arbitrage between trading mechanisms. The models of Brock and Hommes \cite{brock1998heterogeneous} and of Lux and Marchesi \cite{lux1999scaling} show the relevance of heterogeneity in financial dynamics; Angeris and coauthors \cite{angeris2020uniswap} analyze, both formally and through agent-based simulation, how Uniswap tracks a reference price; Campbell and coauthors \cite{campbell2025optimal} study a joint CEX/DEX model for fees and the profitability of liquidity provision; and Bergault and coauthors \cite{bergault2025exit} formulate liquidity withdrawal as an optimal stopping problem. These antecedents prevent presenting the mere combination of a reference market and an AMM as a sufficient novelty. For the system delimited here, what remains is the need to explicitly contrast a low-order dynamical approximation with the behavior generated by execution rules, limited arbitrage and adaptive liquidity.

A two-level experimental framework is proposed. The first represents the decentralized market with a discrete constant-product simulator, an exogenous CEX price and noise, trend, arbitrage and liquidity-provision agents. The second describes the tracking excess error and the net order pressure with a discrete local map of two variables. The parameters of the map are estimated from simulator observables and are not assumed to be universal constants. The contrast is carried out with reference scenarios, exogenous perturbations, variations in arbitrage capacity and depth, and liquidity withdrawal, and it is governed by a confirmatory protocol fixed before simulating the confirmation seeds.

The expectation is to identify configurations in which the discrepancy is corrected within a defined horizon, configurations in which it persists, and conditions under which a lower depth amplifies the response to a perturbation; it is also expected to quantify the error of the reduced map against the simulator and to document the regions in which it is not adequate. Chapter~\ref{cap:resultados} reports the outcome: active arbitrage markedly reduces the excess error and capacity grades the recovery (H1); adaptive liquidity withdrawal amplifies the response only at the lowest depth tested (H2); and the reduced map beats persistence, but not a scalar autoregressive model (H3). All results are synthetic, and the contribution is limited to the conditions that the model represents.

The document is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{cap:problema} delimits the problem and reviews the antecedents; chapter~\ref{cap:justificacion} justifies its relevance. Chapters~\ref{cap:hipotesis} and~\ref{cap:objetivos} present the hypotheses and the objectives. Chapter~\ref{cap:metodologia} develops the mathematical formulation, the rules of the micro-world, the experimental design and the statistical protocol. Chapter~\ref{cap:resultados} presents the analytical and confirmatory results, and chapter~\ref{cap:discusion} discusses them and states the conclusions they support. Chapter~\ref{cap:futuro} proposes future work; the bibliography and the technical annexes (chapter~\ref{cap:anexos}) close the document.
