\chapter{Introduction}
\label{cap:introduccion}

Cryptoassets trade on centralized exchanges (CEX) and decentralized exchanges (DEX), including automated market makers (AMMs) whose quoted prices depend on pool reserves. Arbitrage can move an AMM price toward an external reference, but the amount and speed of that adjustment depend on the swap rule, costs, available reserves, capital and transaction ordering. These mechanisms are studied in the literature on AMM loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR), arbitrage and miner or maximal extractable value (MEV) \cite{milionis2022lvr,daian2020flash,zhou2021hft}. This thesis isolates a much narrower setting: a zero-fee constant-product pool, one exogenous CEX reference and a bounded arbitrage rule.

The first contribution is analytical. A swap of $c$ times the input reserve changes the log price by $2\log(1+c)$ in the direction of the reference, until the no-arbitrage band is reached. This gives an ideal ticks-to-band calculation without simulation. The simulation addresses the two parts of the thesis question within the tested grid: how arbitrage capacity and liquidity depth shape post-shock tracking, and how accurately reduced maps describe the resulting finite-horizon trajectories. Noise, trend and withdrawal rules make these outcomes conditional on this heterogeneous-agent micro-world.

The agent-based model is deliberately small and reproducible. It represents one constant-product pool, an exogenous reference and stylized noise, trend, arbitrage and liquidity-provider rules. The withdrawal rule is triggered in every tested E3 run, so it is treated as a fixed intervention rather than evidence of heterogeneous provider behavior. Depth is varied while absolute order sizes are held fixed in the main design; a separate sensitivity scales orders with reserves. The experiment therefore shows how conclusions depend on the order-to-reserve ratio and panic rule, not how depth alone determines a real market response.

The reduced-model clause of the research question is assessed as predictive description of continuous tracking-error paths on held-out simulator runs. The finite-window labels summarize those paths; they are not independently observed regimes or targets for a classification test. The two-state map does not outperform the scalar autoregression. The band-aware comparison has a small gain concentrated in one synthetic condition and receives the realized future CEX path, so it is a replay diagnostic rather than a real-time forecast. The results do not establish a regime classifier or global stability.

The most useful engineering implication is a simulator-specific measurement result. In E3, with the nine design cells equally weighted, 31.2\,\% of recovered paths later left the no-arbitrage band; the conditional departure rate was 98.8\,\% at depth factor 0.5 and zero at depth factors 1 and 2. A post-hoc peak-error measure also shows an E3 increase only at the shallowest depth under fixed orders, with the withdrawal and reserve-linked arbitrage cap still confounded. These patterns motivate recording first entry, sustained return, later departure and tick-end peak error separately in a future synchronized replay; they are not validated rates or operational requirements for live markets.
